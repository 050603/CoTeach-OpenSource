## Teaching accuracy and continuity

- A standalone course-first page may greet the class briefly, then should enter the actual teaching object without delay. Explain why the learning matters when learners need that orientation, in a natural sentence tied to the knowledge. Do not recite formal objectives or prolong the greeting into a ceremonial introduction.
- A concrete case must show the actions, observations, wording, quantities, or results needed for the inference. Establish what happened and why it matters before naming unfamiliar categories. A setting label alone is not a worked case.
- Give adjacent pages different learning contributions. Reuse earlier material only to add a new relation, mechanism, derivation step, condition, or application, or to restore a premise briefly.
- Keep definitions, features of an example, heuristics, necessary conditions, and recommendations distinct. Do not turn a local comparison or useful option into a universal rule.
- Preserve the strength of supportive causal claims. A source saying that an activity, environment, tool, or intervention helps, improves, coordinates, or makes a process easier does not establish that the process or underlying relationship exists only with that support. In mechanism and misconception explanations, describe the supported improvement; do not infer “only then can it happen”, “without that intervention there is no relationship”, or a one-to-one assignment of different supports to different underlying mechanisms. A teaching recommendation is not evidence of the nonexistence of a cognitive, physical, or technical process. Keep genuine source-established necessary conditions and cite their separate basis when using a necessity claim.
- Preserve conditional direction even within one case: “if A, then B” does not establish what happens without A. Do not turn it into “whether A happens determines whether B happens”, “B is possible only after A”, or “without A, B cannot happen” unless the adopted source or an established principle supplies a separate basis for necessity or uniqueness. Keep this scope in case explanations, condensed slide claims, and assessment criteria.
- When contrasting mechanisms under one broader process, preserve how each can contribute to that process. A defining difference does not make one mechanism ineffective or turn another mechanism's distinguishing feature into a necessary condition for every successful outcome. Derive misconceptions from actual conflicts with the adopted knowledge, rather than inventing a universal rule to make the contrast sharper.
- Replacement, deletion, scope-of-effect, and comparison may help explain a distinction, but they are not sufficient definitions or universal decision rules.
- Preserve the source's subjects, quantifiers, and “or/and” relations. Replacing one component or finding another solution path does not establish that the whole task has no knowledge contribution or falls outside a method. Limit deletion/replacement diagnostics to the stated task, learning objective, and established necessary relations; do not invent an exclusive requirement for one item where the source requires a relevant class of knowledge or skills. Keep genuine source-established necessary conditions.
- Preserve exact terms, facts, units, quantities, and established example details across the section. If a condition changes, name the change and keep other relevant conditions consistent.
- Preserve substantive degree and scope qualifiers across explanations, summaries, transitions, tasks and assessment criteria. A source's qualified claim does not become an absolute claim when condensed into a goal or answer label.
- Planning objectives and understanding criteria select teaching responsibilities; they do not establish new factual claims. Derive first-draft explanations and assessment answers from the adopted original evidence and the knowledge actually taught, preserving the same conditions and qualifications when a planning label is abbreviated.
- Preserve a process's actor, object, phase, and purpose from the adopted source, as well as its complete steps and order. Planning or design checks are not the procedure carried out by learners, and model development is not model inference. When applying a design principle to an activity, explain their relationship instead of relabeling one workflow as another. Keep this distinction in constructed examples, slide summaries, narration, and assessment criteria; use only previously established knowledge in an early application.
- Support conclusions with the premise, evidence, operation, or relationship that makes them follow. A heading, keyword, number of steps, or visual grouping is not a reason by itself.
- Match the explanation to the knowledge. For a concept, unpack unfamiliar terms and boundaries. For a mechanism, connect conditions, intermediate processes, and results. For a derivation, state each consequential step and why it is valid. For a skill, connect actions with the observation or result that tells learners whether the action worked.
- Before asking learners to compare, infer, calculate, judge, or operate, provide the information and prior explanation the task actually requires. Distinguish observations from predictions, expectations, and hypothetical outcomes.
- A concrete example must contain the details needed for the inference it supports. A setting label or category name alone is not a worked example.
- Absence of an explicit rationale, condition, or fact in a written plan establishes only that it was not stated there. It does not prove the underlying claim false or true.
- Source quotations constrain what the source says. Do not attach an additional factual claim to a quotation unless the source or established domain knowledge supports it. Constructed examples or data must not be given a fabricated institution, study, or citation.
- Preserve a source case's actual ending and factual status. A later action, observation, correction, or successful outcome that would help illustrate the theory is not evidence that it happened. Explain the original case as it stands; if a further contrast is needed, use an independent example or an explicitly hypothetical continuation, keeping the hypothetical wording in the explanation, slide, and assessment criteria. Teacher-only provenance notes do not substitute for this logical distinction.
- In the first draft, derive case reasoning from the supplied facts before choosing its theoretical label. Do not invent a missing event to make one story demonstrate every phase of a theory. Keep added illustrative assumptions separate from adopted source facts and register adaptations in the existing reviewItems metadata.
- Preserve the scope of an object's features and exclusions. Lacking one specific structure, function, or form does not imply lacking every related structure or function. Check concrete comparisons against established domain knowledge before using them to support an inference; if the source supplies no observation, do not invent one.
